Franco-Spanish wealth: five questions to ask before crossing the Pyrenees

← All insights

Settling, investing or transmitting between France and Spain raises wealth questions that are not solved country by country, but through an overall reading. Here are five worth asking before you decide.

1. Where will you be tax resident?

This is the question that conditions all the others. Tax residence is not freely chosen: it follows precise criteria — the location of your home, the time spent, the centre of your economic interests — assessed by each country and then settled by the tax treaty between France and Spain. A poorly prepared move can lead to dual residence, or even temporary double taxation. Determining your residence before moving, rather than after, avoids most of the difficulties.

2. How is your real estate held?

A home in Spain, a rental property in France, a civil property company here, direct ownership there: each configuration carries different consequences for income, taxation and, above all, succession. Spanish and French law do not treat real estate the same way, and a structure that is efficient on one side of the Pyrenees can prove costly on the other.

3. Are your investment wrappers suitable?

A life insurance policy taken out in France does not necessarily produce the same effects once residence is transferred to Spain — for tax purposes as well as for transmission. Depending on the situation, other frameworks, French or Luxembourg, may offer greater portability and legal certainty. The point is not to change everything, but to check that your wrappers follow your mobility rather than work against it.

4. Does your transmission account for both countries?

Succession rules — the order of heirs, protection of the spouse, taxation, the ability to choose the applicable law — differ significantly between France and Spain, and even vary from one Spanish autonomous community to another. A transmission designed with only one country in mind can produce unintended effects in the other. Anticipating it years in advance means keeping the choice rather than having it imposed.

5. Who coordinates your advisers?

Lawyer, notary, asesor, accountant, banker: in a cross-border situation, each is competent in their field and their country — but no one, by default, holds the overall view. That coordinating role is precisely what a wealth adviser provides: making the various professionals talk to one another, prioritising decisions and ensuring their coherence over time.

In short

A Franco-Spanish situation is rarely handled well by adding up decisions taken separately. It is the overall logic — residence, real estate, investments, transmission, coordination — that makes the difference.
